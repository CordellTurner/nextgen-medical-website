01 / THE SERVICE

What is hormone therapy?

Hormone therapy uses a prescribed hormone to address a specific health concern after an individual evaluation. “Bioidentical” describes hormones with the same chemical structure as hormones made by the body; the term alone does not mean a product is safer, more effective, or FDA-approved. NeXtGen Medical lists cream and pellet options. The hormone, dose, route, and whether a product is FDA-approved or compounded all matter, and your provider can explain the specific option being considered.

For people experiencing menopause-related symptoms, systemic estrogen therapy may help with bothersome hot flashes and night sweats. Some low-dose vaginal hormone treatments may help with vaginal or urinary symptoms; these are different from systemic treatments and are not interchangeable. Hormone therapy may also be considered in certain other diagnosed hormone-related conditions, but it is not a general treatment for aging, weight loss, or nonspecific symptoms. Whether it may help depends on your symptoms, health history, and personal risk factors.

If you have a uterus, using systemic estrogen typically requires discussion of endometrial protection, often with a progestogen. Not everyone should use hormone therapy. Potential risks vary with the type, dose, route, duration, age, and health history; your provider can talk through the benefits, risks, alternatives, and uncertainties relevant to you.

02 / AVAILABLE OPTIONS

Understand creams, pellets, and other forms.

Hormone medicines come in different forms, and the best fit depends on the condition being treated and your preferences. Some creams are applied to the skin; low-dose vaginal hormone treatments, which may include creams, are a distinct option for certain vaginal or urinary symptoms. Products and absorption differ, so use only the product and route prescribed. Pellets are small implants placed under the skin by a clinician and release hormone over time. Because a pellet cannot be quickly removed or adjusted like a daily dose, ask how dose decisions, side effects, and follow-up are handled.

“Bioidentical” products can include FDA-approved medicines as well as custom-compounded preparations. Compounded medicines are not FDA-approved, and the FDA does not verify their safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. Ask whether the proposed product is FDA-approved or compounded, why that formulation is being recommended, what alternatives are available, and how its dose will be monitored. Read the FDA’s information about compounded drugs.

03 / WHO IT MAY BE FOR

Begin with a conversation about your health.

If you have questions or concerns about hormones, you can discuss them during a consultation. The provider will review your symptoms, medical history, medications, and any relevant testing to consider whether further evaluation or treatment options may be appropriate. Hormone levels alone do not determine whether treatment is needed, and an evaluation does not guarantee treatment. Be sure to mention a history of blood clots, stroke, certain cancers, liver disease, unexplained vaginal bleeding, or other significant health conditions, as these may affect the decision.

04 / WHAT TO EXPECT

Make a plan for treatment and follow-up.

A consultation may include a review of your concerns, health history, and goals, along with an examination or laboratory testing when appropriate. If treatment is considered, discuss what improvement to watch for, how long it may take to assess, possible side effects, and when to check back. Monitoring should be tailored to the treatment and your health; additional testing is not automatically needed for every person.

Before deciding, ask which specific hormone and product are proposed, whether it is FDA-approved or compounded, what evidence supports its use for your concern, what alternatives exist, and what the full cost will be. You can also ask how to contact the practice if you have side effects or want to revisit the plan.
